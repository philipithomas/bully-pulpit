## The Philip test

The site describes an **editorial workshop home**, not a generic luxury box: real wood, metal, ceramics, texture and patina; quiet inside a dense, walkable city; books, records, photography and tinkering; a kitchen that supports actual cooking; and nature close enough to enter the daily routine. The best evidence is in [Lost and found](content/contraption/2025-01-10-lost-and-found.mdx), [Software in the AI era](content/contraption/2026-03-20-software-in-the-ai-era.mdx), [Fresh coat of paint](content/workshop/2026-02-19-fresh-coat-of-paint.mdx), [Digital product studios](content/contraption/2024-01-28-digital-product-studios.mdx), [Roastery](content/tsundoku/2026-06-24-roastery.mdx), [Morning coffee](content/tsundoku/2026-06-29-morning-coffee.mdx), [Build and tinker](content/workshop/2025-12-05-build-and-tinker.mdx), and [Chat with my dog](content/contraption/2025-05-09-chat-with-my-dog.mdx).

“Philip lives here” should mean calm proportions, honest materials, useful built-ins, one memorable architectural move, and windows that can stay open toward trees, sky, or a private courtyard. Generic sameness whose identity comes mainly from a finish package or amenity list earns little credit. A plain apartment with good bones can become Philip-coded; a cramped or badly located one cannot be decorated into the right home.

For this purchase, the taste test is subordinate to four realities:

- Two children must still fit when they are no longer babies. A legal third bedroom is valuable; an interior office or cellar is not scored as one.
- Neighborhood is part of the apartment. A usable dog park route, groceries, cooking ingredients, cafés, and a street worth walking every day matter more than a roof lounge.
- Wall Street matters now and Penn next year. Direct service or one easy transfer beats a theoretically fast multi-connection trip.
- The in-law range should buy a durable step-change. It should not merely purchase newer appliances, a lobby, or 100 extra square feet.

Score bands: **85+ priority tour**, **75–84 strong**, **65–74 conditional or diamond**, and **below 65 skip**. A “diamond” must score at least 70, have exceptional character or a singular living advantage, leave financial room, and have fixable—not structural—weaknesses. Scores measure fit assuming the specifically stated listing facts; any score labeled `potential` remains subordinate to the named verification gate.

Hard gates override the score: Manhattan or Brooklyn only; no purchase above $1.7 million; no third-floor-or-higher unit without a confirmed elevator; no occupancy after October 11; no investor-only or tenant-occupied deal; and no non-condo presented as a condo. Elevator, bedroom legality, pet policy, exact unit identity, and occupancy are verification gates that can pause a tour or offer. Certificate of occupancy, flood/moisture history, sponsor costs, reserves, insurance, and assessments are diligence risks reflected in confidence and financial scoring rather than presumed away.

## Price and carrying-cost rules

- **Core:** up to and including $1.5 million. These should be financeable without in-law participation.
- **Stretch:** above $1.5 million through $1.7 million. These are ranked separately and should deliver a family or quality-of-life step-change.
- **Outside cap:** asking above $1.7 million. These are excluded from the ranking unless the seller gives a credible written indication that a purchase at or below $1.7 million is possible; the purchase price never crosses the cap.
- **⚠ High common charges:** anything over $1,000/month, exactly as requested. “Fixed monthly” below means the listing's current common charges plus stated taxes, before mortgage, insurance, utilities, and any unlisted assessment.
- Sponsor/new-development purchases can shift transfer taxes, sponsor attorney fees, working-capital contributions, and other closing costs to the buyer. Those are not in the fixed-monthly number.
- Tax abatements, primary-residence abatements, seller concessions, and temporary assessments are called out because the headline monthly number may not be the durable one.
- The two-month occupancy window is a hard operational target, not something a live listing proves. Vacancy is necessary but not sufficient; attorney, title, lender, waiver, board and sponsor timing must be confirmed before relying on an October 11 move-in.

## Detailed reading of the leading candidates

### 1. 373 Rutland Road #2A — the family answer

The plan is unusually honest for the price: bedrooms around 16'4" × 10', 12' × 11', and 12' × 8', two full baths, a roughly 21'6" × 12'2" living/dining room, a real walk-in closet, in-unit laundry, and a private elevator entrance. The 55-square-foot balcony is not a dog yard, but the tree-facing windows and short Prospect Park route supply the open-window/green relationship. Bosch appliances and a clean warm palette are good enough now without pretending to be the source of the apartment's identity.

This is farther east than the original center of gravity, but it is a rational expansion: Prospect Park becomes daily infrastructure and the 2/5 serves both Wall Street and Penn without a forced transfer. PLG is architecturally rich and food-oriented, though block-by-block feel matters and the trip is longer than Atlantic Terminal.

**Before touring:** confirm certificate of occupancy and closings timeline; full sponsor buyer costs; pet rules and dog weight/breed limits; actual tax basis; elevator reliability; whether the balcony is private in the offering plan; sound between units; and whether the narrowest bedroom fits the intended bunk/desk/storage plan.

### 2. 41 Putnam Avenue #1 — the garden diamond

The main floor gives both legal bedrooms direct access toward a 390-square-foot rear garden, two baths, and a usable 22-foot living/dining room. The roughly 31' × 18' lower recreation room is the high-upside part: library, Leica editing table, records, workshop, playroom, exercise, and storage can coexist without forcing the main room to do everything. Its neutral sponsor finish is generic but fixable; the garden, arches, volume, and low fixed carrying cost are the bones.

The weakness is equally clear. This is a two-bedroom duplex, not a documented three-bedroom. Daily park access is less immediate than Rutland or Washington, and a garden/cellar unit introduces water, pests, security, privacy, and light questions. A two-child plan works only if the children share the second legal bedroom for years and the lower room remains useful non-sleeping space.

**Before touring:** inspect after rain; get flood and water-intrusion history, drainage/backflow/sump details, cellar legal use and ceiling height, radon/mold/pest records, garden maintenance rights, security, sponsor closing costs, and a furniture plan for both legal bedrooms. Confirm that the virtually staged garden matches the delivered condition.

### 3. 124 Washington Avenue #2C — the compact three-bedroom

The Candy Factory solves more of the explicit checklist than almost anything near Fort Greene: three windowed bedrooms, two baths, W/D, elevator, pets, storage, gym, common garden and roof, plus Fort Greene Park close enough for the dog. The rooms are compact—approximately 14'4" × 10', 10'2" × 9', and 9'2" × 8'10"—but they are more useful to a future family than a glamorous oversized primary plus an interior office. Common charges are exceptionally low at $432.

The $300,000 price cut from $1.8 million is either an opportunity or a warning that the initial sponsor pricing was fantasy. Listing imagery leans on renderings and building/neighborhood material. At 1,152 square feet, the 22' × 11'10" living/dining room and 9' × 7'7" kitchen need a real table/sofa/storage rehearsal. The G-first commute is less elegant than Atlantic Terminal.

**Before touring:** verify the unit is physically complete and can close/occupy inside two months; reconcile renderings with installed finishes; review the offering plan, amendments, reserve fund, sponsor track record, certificate of occupancy, tax lot and buyer closing costs; measure every bedroom; and time the actual Wall/Penn commute at work hours.

### 4. 1 Hanson Place #18L — the identity choice

This is the strongest existing-home evidence of a calm, materially personal interior. Twelve-foot beams, wide western/water views, custom built-ins, soundproof floors, and an acoustically treated primary bedroom make the landmark shell useful rather than merely photogenic. The 1,311-square-foot plan has enough common-room volume and storage to support books, records, photography, a real table, and two children sharing a room. Atlantic Terminal downstairs is the best dual-office transit in the search.

The cost is not hidden: **⚠ $1,888 common charges** plus $1,020 taxes. A seller concession of $250/month for three years lowers the effective fixed cost to about $2,658 temporarily, then it returns to about $2,908; the tax figure also appears to include the primary-residence abatement. There is no credible third-bedroom path without damaging the apartment's reason for existing.

**Before touring:** obtain the last two years of board minutes, financials, insurance, reserve study and assessment history; confirm the exact concession mechanics and post-concession carrying cost; verify primary-residence tax assumptions; test Barclays/Atlantic train and event noise with windows both open and closed; and map two children's storage.

### 5. 354 Dean Street #2 — the Boerum compromise

The address and commute are nearly ideal. Atlantic–Barclays is under 500 feet, common charges and taxes total only $1,111, the floor-through plan has front and rear balconies, and the new kitchen offers quartzite, Monogram/ZLINE appliances, triple-pane windows and central air. A second-floor private entrance satisfies the access rule without an elevator.

The 977-square-foot plan is the problem. Bedrooms around 7'5" × 18'4" and 8'1" × 13'4" are long and narrow, the common room is roughly 10 feet wide, there is no third-room path, and the balconies face nearby buildings rather than delivering true privacy. StreetEasy says “laundry in building” while the plan labels an in-unit laundry closet. A three-unit sponsor condo also concentrates repair and governance risk among very few owners.

**Before touring:** confirm an installed, vented or drain-pan-compliant W/D rather than hookups; tape out a bunk/desk/storage plan; check balcony sightlines and arena/block noise; review sponsor documents, reserves, insurance and owner voting; and confirm pet rights in the offering plan.

### 6. 100 Jay Street #5A — the dog-park 2BR

The J Condominium offers a conventional 1,223-square-foot two-bedroom plan, in-unit laundry, elevator, pets, and Brooklyn Bridge Park as a genuinely excellent daily dog landscape. DUMBO also makes Wall Street unusually easy by foot, train or ferry, while the F provides a usable Penn-area route. The older cherry/black-granite kitchen is dated but has better renovation potential than a tiny glossy new-build galley.

It remains a 2BR with **⚠ $1,169 common charges** and $1,455 taxes. DUMBO's visitor traffic and retail mix are less neighborhood-like than Boerum, Fort Greene or Park Slope, and the fifth-floor exposure must be tested for bridge/traffic noise and privacy.

### 309 East 108th Street #4E — the unscored Manhattan schoolhouse wildcard

At $949,000, this could become the most literal diamond in the search: a claimed 1,750 square feet in a converted schoolhouse, roughly 14-foot double-height volume, wraparound windows, parquet, an eccentric mezzanine rail, three upper bedrooms, two baths, W/D, elevator, pets, and a huge shared garden. The dated kitchen and baths leave more than half a million dollars below the core cap for a measured renovation rather than someone else's flip. Thomas Jefferson Park and Randall's Island create a credible dog/nature routine.

The East Harlem location is a real neighborhood departure, the Lexington line does not serve Penn directly, and **⚠ $1,628 common charges** offset some purchase-price value. More seriously, the live page is #4E while the displayed floor plan says “Apartment 2E.” That may be an innocent reused-plan error or evidence that the advertised layout is not the exact unit.

**Do not tour until the broker sends:** the exact stamped or offering-plan floor plan for #4E, tax lot, legal bedroom count, current-condition video, elevator confirmation, alteration history, certificate of occupancy, assessment history, and an explanation of every prior listing/relist. If those clear, this is the best fixer to see.

### 7. 230 Ashland Place #11D — the rational fallback

The current $1,162,500 ask is down from $1.275 million and leaves meaningful renovation/furniture reserves. The unit has south/west light, W/D, elevator, large closets, two baths, and one of the best transit/park neighborhoods in the search. But **⚠ $1,651 common charges** plus $1,104 taxes produce a $2,755 fixed monthly cost, and the 2010-era kitchen and ordinary tower plan do not have innate character. Buy it only if the exact light, view and furniture plan feel exceptional in person; the price alone is not the identity.

## Showing and diligence checklist

### Before scheduling

- Ask for the offering-plan floor plan, current condition video, exact common charges, current taxes, every assessment, last two years of board minutes/financials, insurance, reserves, pet rules, certificate of occupancy, and target closing/occupancy date.
- For sponsor units, get a line-item buyer closing-cost estimate before treating the ask as comparable with a resale.
- Ask whether every advertised bedroom is legal, windowed, and represented in the offering plan. “Office,” “den,” “recreation room,” and “currently used as a bedroom” receive no automatic bedroom credit.
- Confirm an elevator for every unit above floor two. Do not infer one from a polished lobby or “wheelchair access” tag.
- Confirm the specific dog is allowed. A pet-search filter is not board/bylaw approval.
- Request any known upcoming façade, roof, elevator, Local Law 11, energy, insurance, or capital assessment.

### At the showing

- Bring the dimensions of the sofa, dining table, bed, bunk/crib, desks, shelving, record storage and guest bed. Tape them out after subtracting doors, radiators, circulation and closet swings.
- Turn every light off. Record time, exposure, sky angle, facing distance, street/arena/train noise, and whether private windows can actually remain uncovered.
- Time door-to-usable-grass, not door-to-the edge of a park on a map. Walk the late-night dog route too.
- Rehearse stroller movement from sidewalk through every door, stair, elevator and storage location.
- Run the W/D and dishwasher; inspect electrical service, venting, induction/gas setup, prep surface, pantry and appliance storage. Serious cooking needs more than expensive brand names.
- In a garden or lower-level unit, inspect after rain if possible. Ask about water entry, flood claims, backflow protection, sump/ejector systems, drainage, dehumidification, mold, pests and who maintains the yard.
- In a tiny condo, study governance: three owners sharing a roof failure can be riskier than a well-funded larger building, even with low common charges.

### After the showing

- Model five-year and ten-year carrying cost with common-charge growth, the end of abatements/concessions/assessments, insurance, maintenance and renovation—not just the mortgage payment.
- Have counsel review the offering plan, amendments, board minutes, financials, litigation, insurance deductibles, reserves, title, tax lot, certificate of occupancy, alterations and sponsor obligations.
- Have the lender approve the building as well as the borrower. Small sponsor buildings, high investor concentration, litigation, inadequate reserves or insurance can derail a primary-residence loan.
- Price any “good bones” work with a licensed contractor and building alteration agreement before assuming the budget can absorb it. New kitchens are fixable; missing legal bedrooms, bad light and a bad commute usually are not.

## Recommendation

Start with **373 Rutland, 41 Putnam, and 124 Washington**. They test three genuinely different family theses: real 3BR by the park, 2BR plus garden/studio flex, and compact 3BR in close-in Clinton Hill. Add **1 Hanson** to learn whether landmark character and perfect transit are worth the high carry and permanent 2BR constraint, then **354 Dean** to learn whether Boerum convenience can overcome its narrow plan.

Keep **309 East 108th** as the fixer wildcard, but require exact-unit documents before spending time on it. Keep **230 Ashland** as the rational value fallback. Use **115 Norfolk** only to calibrate the aesthetic, and use the Nevins units only to calibrate operational ease.

In the stretch pool, require more than “nicer.” Tour **164 South Oxford** only if the broker documents the office/flex, its permitted use, and exact dimensions. Tour **11 West 126th** if a private garden plus huge flex is worth changing the Manhattan neighborhood target. Tour **509 Pacific** only after deciding that a beautiful, quiet, cooking-forward 2BR is itself worth family help despite providing no new bedroom.

The clearest present strategy is not “buy the prettiest condo under $1.7 million.” It is: buy the best neighborhood-and-family chassis under $1.5 million, preserve cash to make it yours, and cross the core cap only for a room, garden, or piece of architecture the family will still value ten years from now.
